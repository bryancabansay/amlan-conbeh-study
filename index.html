How we analyzed the buying journey

Lewis asks businesses to start with how customers actually buy. We used the six strands of Customer Buying Journey DNA as the analytical lens.

Strand 1

Triggers

What starts the journey, such as heat, thirst, meals, work, or travel.

Strand 2

Steps

What the buyer actually does from need recognition to purchase.

Strand 3

Key Players

People who influence the purchase, including retailers and family members.

Strand 4

Buying Style

How the buyer approaches the decision, including Lewis's 4Q model.

Strand 5

Value Drivers

What the buyer expects to gain, such as safety, availability, price, taste, or convenience.

Strand 6

Buying Concerns

Friction that can delay, redirect, or stop the purchase.

4Q Buying Style: why “Sort and Select” matters here

The model asks two questions: does the buyer already know what they want, and do they see many acceptable choices or unique value in one supplier or brand?

Choice + Solution

Search and Choose

The buyer needs help deciding but can choose among several providers.

Value + Solution

Trusted Advisor

The buyer needs help and trusts one provider to guide the decision.

Choice + Product

Sort and Select

Closest fit for many single-bottle purchases in our interviews. The buyer already wants water and chooses among acceptable options that are available, cold, safe, and reasonably priced.

Value + Product

“Starbucks”

The buyer knows what they want and strongly prefers one particular brand or supplier.

Why this matters for Amlan: if the consumer is sorting among what is available now, a brand that is absent from the cooler or shelf cannot compete on taste, price, or brand preference.
Framework source: Martyn R. Lewis, How Customers Buy… & Why They Don’t (2018).

Context effect

Coldness becomes more important at the point of consumption.

Heat, work, travel, exercise, meals, and immediate thirst can change which attribute becomes most important. The survey captures stated importance; interviews capture the purchase situation.

Attitude-behavior gap

Sustainability is liked, but often not decisive.

Respondents can genuinely support eco-friendly packaging while still choosing based on price, availability, coldness, safety, or convenience when the purchase happens.

Retailers shape the available choice set

What management sees vs what the field evidence adds

The study does not simply repeat management's view. It broadens the diagnosis by comparing company assumptions with evidence from buyers, retailers, and stores.

Consumer choicePrice point and taste profile

What the field evidence adds

Consumer choicePrice and taste matter, but safety, availability, trust, and coldness also shape the purchase.
Why consumers switchPrice and availability are strongly supported; safety cues can also stop choice.
RetailersRetailers can become de facto brand selectors when shoppers simply ask for “water.”
DistributionThe issue appears broader than capacity: retailer access, supplier contact, visibility, refrigeration, and shelf execution also matter.
Retailer stockingRetailers also consider margin, delivery reliability, demand, reorder ease, and inventory risk.
Interpretation: management appears directionally right, but the field evidence suggests the path-to-purchase problem extends beyond product attributes and production capacity.

Critical reflection: does Lewis fully explain bottled water?

This case is useful partly because it tests the framework in a very short, low-involvement consumer journey.

Where Lewis works well

  • Triggers remain identifiable: heat, thirst, food, work, and travel.
  • Steps still exist, even when the whole journey takes only seconds.
  • Retailers clearly operate as Key Players.
  • Value Drivers and Buying Concerns explain why an acceptable bottle is chosen or rejected.
  • Sort and Select fits many commodity-like, single-bottle purchases.

Where the framework needs caution

  • The book's detailed research and examples are strongly rooted in organizational and B2B buying, even though Lewis presents the framework as broadly applicable.
  • Low-involvement purchases can be far less deliberate than complex organizational purchases.
  • Situational conditions such as heat, immediate thirst, refrigeration, and shelf availability can dominate the decision.
  • Different bottled-water occasions may represent different journeys, so one “average customer” can hide important variation.
Our conclusionLewis still helps explain bottled-water purchasing, but the journey is highly compressed and situational. The framework is most useful here as a diagnostic lens, not as a claim that every purchase follows a long or conscious process.

Managerial implications

These are evidence-based implications, not proof of causality. The next step should be a controlled retail test.

1 · Fix access first

Improve retailer access and replenishment.

A clear retailer contact channel, reliable delivery, and stock tracking address friction before the consumer ever reaches the product.

2 · Win the moment

Strengthen visibility, cold availability, and point-of-purchase access.

For fast, low-involvement purchases, cooler execution may matter as much as communication.

3 · Segment the journey

Different buying situations require different market approaches.

Single-bottle buyers, household bulk buyers, and institutional/event buyers can have different buying DNA and different priorities.

A practical 90-day pilot

Use the exploratory findings to generate measurable evidence before scaling.

Days 1–30

Map and stabilize access

Baseline outlet coverage, fill rate, delivery reliability, supplier contacts, and current shelf/cooler presence.

Days 31–60

Pilot the moment of choice

Secure trial outlets, make reordering easy, improve facings and cold-stock standards, then verify execution.

Days 61–90

Measure and learn

Track sell-through, stockouts, reorder rate, trial, on-shelf availability, and cold availability before deciding what to scale.

Limitations and boundaries

These constraints define what the study can and cannot claim.

  • Convenience sample: survey percentages are descriptive of this sample, not population estimates for Metro Cebu or the Philippines.
  • Small qualitative sample: six consumer and six retailer interviews explain patterns, not prevalence.
  • Store-photo scope: absence from a photographed display does not prove a store did not stock Amlan elsewhere.
  • No institutional-buyer interviews: LGU, corporate, and event journeys remain under-researched.
  • No competitor-price audit: the study cannot independently establish that Amlan is the cheapest option.
  • Stated vs actual behavior: survey attitudes can differ from decisions made under real purchase conditions.

Interpretation boundary
The website presents an exploratory consumer-behavior study. It identifies recurring patterns and managerial hypotheses that should be tested further rather than treated as causal or population-level conclusions.
Central conclusion

Amlan appears to face a path-to-purchase problem before it faces a product-preference problem. Many consumers may not reach the point where they can evaluate the brand at all.

A brand cannot win a decision it never gets the chance to join.

Interpret carefully

Exploratory convenience sample. Findings describe the participants and observed outlets in this project. They should not be generalized to all Filipino consumers or all retailers.
